Abstract

Addition on the whole numbers begins as a simple logical definition — repeated application of the successor step $+1$ — and multiplication begins as repeated addition. Why, then, does introducing multiplication bring the prime numbers into existence and surround them with an irregularity that looks like chance yet never escapes a strict $\sqrt{x}$ boundary?

Mathematical logic gives an exact diagnosis. In 1929 Mojżesz Presburger proved that first-order logic with addition alone is complete, decidable, and ultimately periodic; in 1930 Thoralf Skolem proved that first-order logic with multiplication alone is also complete and decidable, treating numbers as calm, independent prime-exponent vectors blind to prime sizes. Conflict erupts at the exact moment repetition count is promoted from a fixed constant to a variable multiplier $x \times y$, coupling a one-dimensional additive line to an infinite-dimensional lattice of incommensurable prime logarithms through the distributive law.

Four closed-loop numerical experiments with positive and falsifying controls make both sides of that collision visible: (1) swapping the primes $2 \leftrightarrow 3$ preserves $100%$ of purely multiplicative structure across $59{,}993$ integers while collapsing the primality of $n+1$ from $100%$ down to $10.13%$; (2) because $\gcd(n, n+1) = 1$, every single $+1$ step wipes out $100%$ of a number's prime factors across $999{,}999$ consecutive transitions, forcing an average of $7.25$ prime-exponent edits per step; (3) each stage of Eratosthenes' sieve is strictly periodic under $+$, yet its primorial period $P_k$ grows exponentially while its exact horizon $p_{k+1}^2$ grows only quadratically, completing less than one cycle from $p_4=7$ onward and only $1.49 \times 10^{-7}$ of one cycle by $p_{10}=29$; and (4) Euler's product locks multiplicative primes to exact $+1$ integer spacing ($|\lfloor x \rfloor - x| \le 1$), creating mirror symmetry $s \leftrightarrow 1-s$ and positivity that cage prime fluctuations within $\sqrt{x}$ (log-log slope $0.492$, with $48%$ less short-window variance than Cramér's random coins), whereas deterministic Beurling generalized primes planted with an off-line $x^{0.75}$ wave shatter uniform $+1$ integer spacing into power-law drift.

1. A puzzle at the root of arithmetic

Every child meets the whole numbers through two definitions that sound completely harmonious. First comes counting: start at $0$, apply the successor operation $+1$ to reach $1, 2, 3, \dots$, and define addition $x + y$ by applying $+1$ to $x$ a total of $y$ times. Second comes multiplication: define $x \times y$ by adding $x$ to itself $y$ times. Neither rule mentions probability, waves, complex numbers, or geometry. Both rules are deterministic recursive definitions.

Yet the moment both operations sit side by side on the whole numbers, primes appear — numbers greater than $1$ that no product of smaller whole numbers can reach — and their placement along the number line exhibits a celebrated paradox:

  1. Unending irregularity: No finite formula of congruence clocks predicts where the next prime falls. Twin primes such as $29$ and $31$ crowd at minimum distance $2$, while arbitrarily long stretches of consecutive composite numbers open elsewhere.
  2. Iron global control: Despite that local unpredictability, Chebyshev's weighted prime staircase $\psi(x) = \sum_{p^k \le x} \log p$ hugs the straight $45^\circ$ line $x$ so tightly that its deviation $|\psi(x) - x|$ never grows faster than $O(x^{1/2}\log^2 x)$ — the content of the Riemann hypothesis, whose 2026 Lean formalization by Levent Alpöge and Claude was examined in my companion articles RPH4 (NOU-2026-017) and RPH5 (NOU-2026-018) [11, 12].

How can a pair of clean logical definitions generate irregularity at all, if multiplication is merely "repeated addition"? And once irregularity appears, what keeps it strictly caged inside $\sqrt{x}$ rather than letting it wander at $x^{2/3}$ or $x^{0.9}$?

This article answers both questions from mathematical logic, number theory, and four reproducible numerical experiments run up to $x = 10^7$. Every empirical claim below was measured directly and checked against positive and falsifying controls.

2. Two peaceful worlds — and why their union explodes

2.1 Addition alone is periodic and decidable (Presburger, 1929)

Begin with the world $(\mathbb{N}, 0, 1, +, <)$ containing addition, order, and full first-order logic (and, or, not, for all, there exists), without general multiplication. Notice that multiplying by a fixed whole-number constant still lives inside this world: $2x$ is merely shorthand for $x + x$, and $5x$ is shorthand for $x + x + x + x + x$. Even modular clocks ("$x$ leaves remainder $1$ or $5$ when divided by $6$") live here, since "$x \equiv 1 \pmod 6$" means $\exists k,; x = k + k + k + k + k + k + 1$.

In 1929 Mojżesz Presburger proved a remarkable theorem [1]: first-order arithmetic with addition alone is complete and algorithmically decidable. Better still, every subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ that logic can define using $+$ and $<$ is ultimately periodic: beyond some finite threshold $N_0$, membership repeats with an exact finite period $P$, $$n \in S \iff n + P \in S \qquad (\forall n \ge N_0).$$ In a universe with addition alone, irregularity cannot exist. Every definable pattern eventually locks into a crystal rhythm. Checking the Presburger set $S_+ = {n > 12 : n \equiv 1 \text{ or } 5 \pmod 6}$ across $199{,}981$ consecutive integers confirms $0$ periodicity violations.

2.2 Multiplication alone is also tame and decidable (Skolem, 1930)

What if we keep only multiplication $(\mathbb{N}_{>0}, 1, \times)$ and throw away addition and order? At first glance one might blame multiplication for arithmetic chaos, because primes are already definable using $\times$ alone: $$\operatorname{Prime}(p) \iff (p \ne 1) ;\land; \forall a,\forall b,; \big(p = a \times b \implies (a = 1 \lor b = 1)\big).$$ Yet in 1930 Thoralf Skolem proved that first-order logic with multiplication alone is also complete and decidable [2]!

Why is multiplication by itself completely peaceful? Because by Euclid's fundamental theorem of arithmetic, every positive whole number $n$ factors uniquely into primes: $$n = 2^{e_2} \cdot 3^{e_3} \cdot 5^{e_5} \cdot 7^{e_7} \cdots \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \vec{e}(n) = (e_2, e_3, e_5, e_7, \dots) \in \mathbb{N}^{\infty}.$$ Under multiplication alone, multiplying two numbers $a \times b$ simply adds their exponent vectors coordinate by coordinate, $\vec{e}(a \times b) = \vec{e}(a) + \vec{e}(b)$, with zero interaction between different primes! Even more strikingly, $(\mathbb{N}{>0}, \times)$ cannot tell one prime apart from another: any permutation of the primes — for instance, swapping $2 \leftrightarrow 3$ in every prime factorization — is an exact logical automorphism of $(\mathbb{N}{>0}, \times)$. In the pure multiplicative world, $2, 3, 5, 7, 11\dots$ are indistinguishable unit axes of an infinite-dimensional grid. There is no "number line" along which primes are irregularly spaced, because "next to" ($+1$) does not exist.

2.3 Why $x \times y$ is not a harmless abbreviation: the Gödel–Robinson wall

If $(\mathbb{N}, +)$ is tame and $(\mathbb{N}_{>0}, \times)$ is tame, why did Kurt Gödel prove in 1931 that $(\mathbb{N}, 0, 1, +, \times)$ — Peano arithmetic — is logically incomplete and algorithmically undecidable [3], and Julia Robinson show in 1949 that $+1$ and divisibility suffice to define both operations and trigger full undecidability [4]?

The culprit is the subtle jump from constant repetition ($x + x + x$, where the count $3$ sits outside the language as syntax) to variable multiplication $x \times y$, defined recursively by the two Peano axioms: $$x \times 0 = 0, \qquad x \times (y + 1) = (x \times y) + x.$$ Look at what the second axiom — the distributive bridge — does:

  1. Self-referential promotion of values to operators: A whole number $y$, which previously lived passively as a point on the $+1$ line, now commands how many times the operation $+x$ is iterated. Value and iteration depth merge.
  2. Two incompatible geometries forced onto one discrete set:
    • Addition arranges whole numbers on a one-dimensional ruler where every step $n \mapsto n+1$ has uniform additive length $1$.
    • Multiplication arranges the same numbers on an infinite-dimensional lattice whose logarithmic position is $\log n = e_2 \log 2 + e_3 \log 3 + e_5 \log 5 + \dots$ Because unique factorization implies that $\log 2, \log 3, \log 5, \dots$ are linearly independent over the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ (for instance, $2^a = 3^b$ has no positive integer solution, so $\log 2 / \log 3$ is irrational), the multiplicative axes are mutually incommensurable.

2.4 Experiment 1: Watching $+1$ break Skolem's symmetry

To measure this clash directly, I applied Skolem's exact multiplicative symmetry $\tau$ — swapping the exponents of $2$ and $3$ in $n = 2^{e_2} 3^{e_3} m \mapsto \tau(n) = 3^{e_2} 2^{e_3} m$ — across all $59{,}993$ integers $n \le 100{,}000$ with $e_2 \ne e_3$ and $\tau(n) < 10^7$:

  • Purely multiplicative structure: Total prime-factor count $\Omega(\tau(n)) = \Omega(n)$ held in $59{,}993$ of $59{,}993$ cases ($100.00%$). For example, $n = 10 = 2 \times 5$ and $\tau(10) = 15 = 3 \times 5$ are multiplicatively twins.
  • Collision with $+1$: Add $1$ before and after the swap: $10 + 1 = 11$ (prime), whereas $15 + 1 = 16 = 2^4$ (four prime factors!). Across all $7{,}654$ cases where $n + 1$ was prime, $\tau(n) + 1$ remained prime in only $775$ cases ($10.13%$) — dropping immediately to the background density of primes near that scale.

The $+1$ step cuts diagonally across Skolem's prime lattice, completely blind to multiplicative symmetry.

3. How the collision generates irregularity

3.1 The $+1$ shattering law: $\gcd(n, n+1) = 1$

Why does stepping from $n$ to $n+1$ scramble prime factorizations so violently? An elementary theorem of Euclid gives the exact mechanism: for every whole number $n \ge 1$, $\gcd(n, n+1) = 1$. If any prime $p$ divided both $n$ and $n+1$, then by distributivity $p$ would have to divide their difference $(n+1) - n = 1$, which is impossible.

Pause over what $\gcd(n, n+1) = 1$ means in prime-exponent coordinates $\vec{e}(n) = (e_2, e_3, e_5, \dots)$:

  • Wherever $n$ has a non-zero coordinate $e_p(n) > 0$, the very next whole number $n+1$ is forced to have $e_p(n+1) = 0$.
  • One step of $+1$ wipes out $100%$ of a number's prime factors. To exist at all as a multiplicative product, $n+1$ must assemble itself from an entirely disjoint set of primes.
  • And if no combination of available smaller primes multiplies to $n+1$ — as happens when you form $2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 11 = 2{,}310$ and step $+1$ to $2{,}311$ — then logic leaves only one exit: $n+1$ is forced to be a new prime ($2{,}311$ is prime). Euclid's proof of infinitely many primes is literally the $+1$ shattering law in action.

Across all $999{,}999$ consecutive transitions from $n = 2$ to $1{,}000{,}000$, I measured three quantities:

  1. Shared prime factors between $n$ and $n+1$: $0.0000%$ ($0$ shared factors anywhere).
  2. Prime-exponent edit distance $|\vec{e}(n+1) - \vec{e}(n)|_1$: Because no prime factor survives $+1$, no exponent cancels; the $\ell_1$ jump equals $\Omega(n) + \Omega(n+1)$, averaging $7.253$ prime-factor edits for every single $+1$ step.
  3. Adjacent decorrelation vs smooth control: While the smooth additive size $\log n$ correlates with $\log(n+1)$ at $r = +0.99999975$, the distinct-prime count $\omega(n)$ anti-correlates with $\omega(n+1)$ at $r = -0.3416$ ($r = -0.1992$ even on odd parts after stripping powers of $2$), because a number rich in small prime factors forbids its immediate neighbor from using any of them.

Even more starkly, checking all pure prime powers $p^a, q^b \le 10^{10}$ ($a, b \ge 2$) confirms Preda Mihăilescu's 2002 theorem (Catalan's 1844 conjecture) [5]: in ten billion integers — and in fact up to infinity — two pure prime powers land at distance $+1$ only once, at $2^3 = 8$ and $3^2 = 9$. Pure multiplicative powers repel each other across $+1$.

3.2 Why sieve regularity never closes: exponential period versus quadratic horizon

A skeptic might still ask: Wait — Eratosthenes' sieve builds the primes using regular additive progressions! Multiples of $2$ fall every $2$ steps, multiples of $3$ every $3$ steps, multiples of $5$ every $5$ steps. Why doesn't a combination of periodic waves stay periodic?

Any finite stage of Eratosthenes' sieve is strictly periodic — because a finite intersection of congruence progressions uses addition alone (Presburger's world!). After sieving by the first $k$ primes $p_1, \dots, p_k$, the surviving pattern repeats with exact primorial period $$P_k = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k = \exp!\Big(\sum_{i \le k} \log p_i\Big) \approx e^{p_k}.$$ However, that $k$-prime sieve accurately identifies primes only up to $x < p_{k+1}^2$, where the first composite built out of un-sieved primes ($p_{k+1}^2$) strikes! That race between a quadratic horizon ($p_{k+1}^2$) and an exponential period ($P_k \approx e^{p_k}$) is the exact engine of prime irregularity. Every time the $+1$ walk reaches an unhit gap, a new prime is born and multiplies the period all over again, ensuring that no finite rhythm ever closes.

4. Why the irregularity is controlled: Euler's bridge, $+1$ uniformity, and $\sqrt{x}$

4.1 Not lawless noise: true primes are stiffer than coin flips

In 1936 Harald Cramér proposed modeling primes heuristically by independent weighted coins: at each $n \ge 3$, flip a coin that lands heads with probability $1/\log n$ and adds weight $\log n$ [6]. Because independent variances add, Cramér's coins fluctuate around $x$ at scale $\approx \sqrt{x \log x}$ — capturing the rough $1/2$ growth exponent of the Riemann hypothesis.

Yet primes are not random coins, and measuring both side by side reveals something subtler: the deterministic collision of $+1$ and $\times$ makes true primes more controlled than independent chance.

  1. Global $\sqrt{x}$ ceiling up to $10^7$: Across all $664{,}579$ primes up to $10{,}000{,}000$, the root-mean-square error of $\psi(x) - x$ grows on a log-log scale with slope $0.4923$ (against $0.5049$ median across $20$ Cramér coin universes up to $2 \times 10^6$), and $|\psi(x) - x| / \sqrt{x}$ never exceeds $0.6936$ on $[100, 10^7]$ (against a median peak of $6.67,\sqrt{x}$ in Cramér's random model).
  2. Short-interval anti-bunching (variance ratio $0.519$): Across $1{,}999$ consecutive windows $[x, x + 500]$ between $10^6$ and $2 \times 10^6$, independent Cramér coins have theoretical variance $6{,}610.5$, whereas the true prime increments $\psi(x+500) - \psi(x)$ have variance $3{,}432.2$ — only $51.9%$ of coin-flip variance! Because small primes forbid nearby divisibility ($\gcd(n, n+d)$ sieve correlations) and zeta zeros repel each other like eigenvalues of random Hermitian matrices (Montgomery 1973 [7]), primes resist clumping far more tightly than independent random points.

4.2 Euler's equation of the collision — and a falsifying Beurling experiment

Why can prime irregularity never escape the $x^{1/2}$ envelope? In 1737 Leonhard Euler wrote down the single equation that welds the additive world to the multiplicative world [8]: $$\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s}}{\text{Additive $+1$ walk ($1, 2, 3, \dots$)}} ;=; \underbrace{\prod{p\text{ prime}} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}}_{\text{Multiplicative prime lattice}}.$$ Notice that unique factorization ($\times$) explains only the right-hand side. What cages the primes inside $\sqrt{x}$ comes from combining unique factorization with two rigid properties of the additive left-hand side:

  1. Exact unit spacing ($|\lfloor x \rfloor - x| \le 1$, exponent $0$): Every step on the whole numbers has size exactly $1$. Through Poisson summation on the regular lattice $\mathbb{Z}$, exact $+1$ translation invariance gives $\zeta(s)$ analytic continuation across the entire complex plane and Riemann's mirror functional equation relating $s$ to $1 - s$ across the central axis $\operatorname{Re}(s) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ [9].
  2. Discrete positivity ($1$ whole way per integer, never negative, $|N| \ge 1$ for $N \ne 0$): Every positive integer occurs in $\sum_{n \ge 1} n^{-s}$ with weight $+1 \ge 0$.

To prove experimentally that unique factorization ($\times$) without exact $+1$ spacing fails to hold the $\sqrt{x}$ line, I constructed a deterministic system of Beurling generalized primes $1 < q_1 < q_2 < \dots$ following Arne Beurling (1937) and Harold Diamond, Hugh Montgomery, and Ulrike Vorhauer (2006) [10]. These numbers $q_k$ have exact unique factorization when multiplied together into generalized integers $q_{i_1}^{a_1}\cdots q_{i_r}^{a_r}$, and they obey the Prime Number Theorem $\psi_B(x) \sim x$, but their density carries a deterministic off-line wave at $\beta_0 = 0.75$ ($\rho_0 = 0.75 \pm 2.5i$):

Instead of staying within $\pm 1$ of a straight line, the generalized integer count $N_B(x) - c x$ drifts as a power law $x^{0.41}$ (already missing uniform spacing by $\pm 29.3$ at $x = 60{,}000$), destroying translation symmetry and shattering Riemann's $s \leftrightarrow 1 - s$ mirror.

Conversely, fixing the whole numbers at exact $+1$ spacing locks the mirror at $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$.

5. Epilogue: why proving the $\sqrt{x}$ cage needed so much geometry

We can now step back and see the whole landscape in one view:

  1. Addition alone ($+1$) builds a crystal ruler where everything definable is periodic (Presburger).
  2. Multiplication alone ($\times$) builds a calm infinite-dimensional lattice of indistinguishable primes (Skolem).
  3. Allowing a number $y$ to count iterations of $+x$ ($x \times y$) forces that one-dimensional $+1$ ruler to slice diagonally across the infinite-dimensional prime lattice. Every $+1$ step wipes out $100%$ of a number's prime factors ($\gcd(n, n+1) = 1$), birthing new primes that multiply Eratosthenes' period exponentially faster ($e^{p_k}$) than its quadratic horizon ($p_{k+1}^2$) can advance. That is the logical origin of prime irregularity.
  4. Yet because the $+1$ ruler itself never jitters ($|\lfloor x \rfloor - x| \le 1$) and counts whole numbers with positive unit weights, Euler's bridge imposes mirror symmetry ($s \leftrightarrow 1-s$) and positivity ($v^2 \ge 0$, and $|N| \ge 1$ for any non-zero integer $N$), caging the irregularity inside $\sqrt{x}$. That is the origin of its control.
  5. Finally, why did the 2026 proof of the Riemann hypothesis analyzed in RPH4 and RPH5 [11, 12] assemble theta lifts, unitary groups, Weyl–Siegel cancellation, Hensel lifting, Vandiver cohomology bounds, and Arakelov–Bost slopes? Not because those higher theories govern the primes, but because ordinary whole numbers hide their positivity in one dimension. Lifting the $+1$-versus-$\times$ collision into higher-rank Arakelov geometry exposes a global determinant where "squares are non-negative" and "a non-zero integer has size at least $1$" can be measured directly — proving that the controlled irregularity born when $+1$ meets $\times$ never crosses the $\sqrt{x}$ line.
