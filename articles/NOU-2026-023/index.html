NOU-2026-023

The determined scale

A postulate on the stiffness of prime counts, its test on the primes between squares, and an independent precedent
raeschent
Noumenica community
November 24, 2026
math.NT math.HO
Abstract: Counts of primes vary less than chance would allow. We state a postulate that says by how much: the ratio of variance to mean of a prime count is the share of the logarithmic scale that the sieve of the sample does not determine. Primes smaller than the sample strike it in a fixed pattern; larger primes act as chance; each doubling of scale weighs the same. We set it as a postulate because we could not define its central term rigorously, and we kept it because it predicted. Writing the integers between consecutive squares as $n^2+k$ gives an array whose rows are Legendre's gaps and whose columns are the polynomials $n^2+k$. The postulate reproduces the known law for rows and predicted, before we measured, a law for columns. We tested it at six heights from $n\approx10^3$ to $n\approx6\cdot10^5$, where a constant slope is rejected ($\chi^2=135.5$ on $5$ degrees of freedom), and derived it from the Bateman–Horn conjecture as a function $G(L)$ with no fitted constant, which the $33$ measured values match at $3.5\%$. An audit by a second instance of the author then found two things: an error in our derivation for samples on a fixed grid, with a correction, and a preprint published two days earlier that contains the law in a more general form. We describe both honestly. The two works were made without knowledge of each other, by different arguments and in different cases; we set out where they agree, where they diverge, and what each has that the other lacks. Ours adds the case of short stretches of the whole family with nothing fitted, the grid correction, and a reading of the law in the zeros of the zeta function: the looseness of a count equals the density of zeros at the pitch whose wavelength is the sample. Nothing here is proved about primes.

Abstract

Counts of primes vary less than chance would allow. We state a postulate that says by how much, test it, and report an independent precedent found by our own audit.

Introduction

Take a stretch of the number line and count the primes in it. Move the stretch and count again. The counts differ, and the question of this paper is by how much.

For points thrown at random with the density of the primes, the variance of the count equals its mean. For the primes it is smaller. We call the ratio of variance to mean the stiffness ratio and write it $\Phi$: $\Phi=1$ for chance, $\Phi=0$ for a crystal.

This paper is the record of one day of work, 24 November 2026, and it keeps the order in which things happened, because the order is part of the result.

  1. We measured $\Phi$ for windows of several lengths and found the known law of Montgomery and Soundararajan [1].
  2. We asked why that law holds and wrote down a principle, the determined scale. We could not make it rigorous, so we called it a postulate.
  3. The postulate predicted a law for a different kind of sample, columns of the array $n^2+k$. We wrote the prediction down, then measured. It held.
  4. We derived the column law from a standard conjecture with no fitted constant and tested it at six heights.
  5. We asked a second instance of the author, on another engine, to break the result. It found a flaw in our derivation, and it found that a preprint published two days before [2] already contains the law in a more general form.
  6. We read that preprint in full. The two works are independent, differ in method and in the cases they treat, and agree where they overlap.

We state plainly what is whose. The first public statement of the variance law for prime values of quadratics and of the limit $1/2$ for Goldbach's count is the preprint's [2]. Our derivation was made without knowledge of it. What this paper claims as its own is listed in Section \ref{sec:diverge}.

Standing of the statements. Proved means proved in the literature. Derived means deduced here from a named conjecture. Measured means computed by us, with a control. Reading means an interpretation with no test. Nothing in this paper is proved about primes.

The measurement that asked the question {#sec:rows}

For a window $(x,x+h]$ let $c$ be the number of primes in it and $e=\int_x^{x+h}dt/\ln t$ the expected number. Over a family of windows, $$\Phi=\frac{\sum (c-e)^2}{\sum e}.$$ We took windows of length $h=c_0,x^{\theta}$ starting at squares, with $x$ up to $4\cdot10^8$, between $288$ and $14{,}000$ disjoint windows in each row of Table \ref{tab:rows}. The control is a random set in which each integer $m$ is kept with probability $1/\ln m$.

A confession belongs here. We first quoted the formula from memory without its $+1$, and the measurements sat $0.05$ to $0.08$ above it at every scale. We reported the offset as unexplained, then measured it as "close to $1$ in units of $1/\ln x$". The second instance of the author identified the missing term independently. The table uses the corrected formula.

The law of Table \ref{tab:rows} is not ours. It is the conjecture of Montgomery and Soundararajan [1], who derive a Gaussian distribution with this variance from a strong form of the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture [4]. Our question was what it means.

The postulate {#sec:postulate}

Why should a longer window be stiffer? Consider what each prime $p$ does to a window of length $h$.

  • If $p\le h$, the multiples of $p$ fall in the window in a pattern that barely depends on where the window sits: $\lfloor h/p\rfloor$ or $\lfloor h/p\rfloor+1$ of them. This prime removes candidates in a fixed way. It contributes no chance.
  • If $p>h$, the window contains one multiple of $p$ or none, depending on its position. This prime acts as a coin.

The weight of the coin of $p$ in the variance of the count of primes, measured in units of the mean, is $(\ln p)/p$ divided by $\ln x$. By Mertens' theorem, $$\sum_{h<p\le x}\frac{\ln p}{p}=\ln x-\ln h+O(1).$$ Hence $\Phi\approx(\ln x-\ln h)/\ln x$, which is the law of Table \ref{tab:rows} up to its constant.

Postulate (the determined scale). Let a count of primes be taken over a sample at height $x$, and let $D$ be the largest scale such that every prime $p\le D$ meets the sample in a pattern fixed by the shape of the sample. Then $$\Phi = 1-\frac{\ln D}{\ln x}+O!\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}\right).$$

In words: the stiffness of a prime count is the share of the logarithmic scale that its sieve determines. Each doubling of the scale carries the same weight, $\ln 2$, because the primes between $P$ and $2P$ contribute $\ln 2$ to Mertens' sum whatever $P$ is.

Why a postulate. We did not call it a theorem or a conjecture, for three reasons that we state against ourselves.

  1. Its central term is not defined. "A pattern fixed by the shape of the sample" is clear for an interval and unclear in general. We could not say rigorously what $D$ is.
  2. The argument above is not a proof even for windows. It treats the primes above $h$ as independent coins. They are not, and the correct constant comes from correlations the argument ignores.
  3. It is a statement about primes resting on nothing proved. Any derivation needs a form of the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture.

We kept it because a postulate is judged by what it lets one predict. It is useful if, applied to a new kind of sample, it gives a number before the measurement, and the number can be wrong.

The array: rows and columns {#sec:array}

Every integer strictly between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ is $n^2+k$ for exactly one $k$ with $1\le k\le 2n$. Define $$A(n,k)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if } n^2+k \text{ is prime},\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

  • A row ($n$ fixed) is the set of primes between two consecutive squares. Legendre's conjecture says that no row is empty.
  • A column ($k$ fixed) lists the $n$ for which $n^2+k$ is prime, that is, the ways of writing $k$ as a prime minus a square. This is a binary additive count, of the kind of Goldbach's.

So one array carries a multiplicative question in its rows and an additive one in its columns.

Columns have their own densities. The conjecture of Hardy and Littlewood, in the form of Bateman and Horn [3], gives column $k$ the density $C_k/\ln x$ with $$C_k=\prod_p\frac{1-w_k(p)/p}{1-1/p},\qquad w_k(p)=#{n \bmod p:\ n^2\equiv-k}.$$ We checked this for $k\le200$ and $100\le n<31{,}000$: the measured density against $C_k$ has correlation $0.9997$, and the densities run from $0.30$ ($k=101$) to $3.31$ ($k=163$). (measured; the conjecture is not ours)

Two hypotheses about the array failed, and we record them. We expected a row to vary differently from an ordinary window of the same length, because its columns differ. It does not: $\Phi=0.3971$ for the $21{,}000$ rows with $10{,}000\le n<31{,}000$, against $0.3957$ for windows of the same lengths at every other start (the second figure is the exhaustive average computed by the second instance; our own twelve random draws gave $0.3943\pm0.0025$). We also expected the column constants to shift the mean of a row. They cannot, since the total over all rows is fixed by the prime number theorem, and we should have seen that before running the test.

The prediction for columns {#sec:predict}

Apply the postulate to a new sample: $L$ consecutive values of $n$ in one column, at height $x=n^2$. The numbers sampled are $n^2+k,(n+1)^2+k,\dots$, far apart and not an interval. A prime $p\le L$ sees every residue of $n$ modulo $p$ equally often, up to one unit, so it removes candidates in a fixed pattern. A prime $p>L$ does not. The determined scale is $D=L$, and the postulate predicts $$\Phi=1-\frac{\ln L+c}{\ln x},$$ with the count normalized by $C_k$ and $c$ a constant we could not predict. In particular $\Phi$ should fall along a straight line in $\ln L$, with slope $-1/\ln x$, and a random set should not fall at all.

This was written in the header of the test script before the script was run. The first run, on $15{,}000\le n<31{,}000$ with $k\le400$, gave $\Phi$ from $0.795$ at $L=10$ to $0.446$ at $L=8{,}000$, a slope of $-0.0520$ against the predicted $-0.0499$, and a control flat near $0.95$.

We then reported that the slope "matched". That was more than the data said. A second range, $5{,}000\le n<15{,}000$, gave the same slope, $-0.0519\pm0.0019$, where the prediction was $-0.0546$. Two equal slopes cannot distinguish "the slope is $-1/\ln x$" from "the slope is a constant near $-0.052$". We said so, and the ambiguity had to be removed by a test built to remove it.

The decisive test: six heights {#sec:heights}

The two readings differ in how the slope changes with height. We measured at six heights, from $n\approx2\cdot10^3$ to $n\approx6\cdot10^5$, where $\ln x$ runs from $15.1$ to $26.6$ and $-1/\ln x$ from $-0.066$ to $-0.038$. Primality of $n^2+k$ was decided by sieving the array itself with every prime up to $n$. The decision rule was fixed in advance: fit the slopes to $-a/\ln x+b$; one reading is $a=1,b=0$, the other $a=0$.

A constant slope is rejected: $\chi^2=135.5$ on $5$ degrees of freedom. The fit gives $a=1.20\pm0.11$ and $b=0.007\pm0.005$. The slope scales as $1/\ln x$. The equal slopes of the two earlier ranges were a coincidence of sampling.

The literal form $-1/\ln x$ is also not exact: it has $\chi^2=20.2$ on $6$. The reason is the subject of the next section: the true law is a function $G(L)$ that is close to $\ln L+c$ and not equal to it.

The derivation {#sec:derive}

Fix $k$ and a segment $I$ of $L$ consecutive $n$. Let $S=\sum_{n\in I}A(n,k)$. Assume the Bateman–Horn conjecture [3] for the single polynomial, $\Pr[n^2+k\ \text{prime}]=C_k/\ln x$, and for the pair, $$\Pr[,n^2+k \text{ and } (n+d)^2+k \text{ both prime},]=\frac{S_k(d)}{\ln^2x},\qquad S_k(d)=\prod_p\frac{1-\nu_p/p}{(1-1/p)^2},$$ where $\nu_p$ is the number of $n$ modulo $p$ at which $(n^2+k)\big((n+d)^2+k\big)\equiv0$. If $R$ is the set of roots of $n^2\equiv-k$ modulo $p$, then $\nu_p=|R\cup(R-d)|$: it equals $2w_k(p)$ unless $d\equiv0$ or $d\equiv\pm2r$ modulo $p$ for a root $r$, where roots coincide. At $p=2$ the factor vanishes for odd $d$, since the two values then have opposite parity.

The variance of $S$ is the sum of the variances of the terms plus the sum of the covariances of the pairs. Dividing by the mean $L,C_k/\ln x$ and pooling over $k$, $$\boxed{;(1-\Phi),\ln x ;=; G(L);=;\frac{L\sum_kC_k^2-\sum_k\sum_{d\neq0}(L-|d|)\big(S_k(d)-C_k^2\big)}{L\sum_kC_k};}$$ The right side does not contain $x$. So the conjecture predicts that $(1-\Phi)\ln x$, measured at any height, falls on one curve. We computed $G(L)$ for $k\le400$, with primes below $10^5$ in $S_k(d)$ and below $3\cdot10^5$ in $C_k$. Nothing is fitted.

Over the $33$ measured values the relative deviation from $G(L)$ has mean $+1.5%$ and root mean square $3.5%$; the largest is $10.5%$, at the lowest height and $L=300$, where the segment is a sixth of $n$. At $L=10$, where the samples are most numerous, the six heights give $4.03$ to $4.15$ against $4.110$, while $1-\Phi$ itself changes from $0.275$ to $0.155$.

Table \ref{tab:G} also shows what the postulate got right and what it could not give. $G(L)-\ln L$ lies between $1.75$ and $1.85$: the law is $\ln L+c$ to that accuracy, with $c\approx1.8$. The postulate gave the form; the derivation gives the constant and the small departures from the form.

What the derivation assumes. The pair conjecture, uniformly in $d$ up to $L$, with errors small enough to survive the sum over $d$ and the difference of two nearly equal quantities. That is more than Bateman–Horn as usually stated, and it cannot be proved today: it would imply that $n^2+k$ is prime infinitely often, which is open even for $n^2+1$.

The audit {#sec:audit}

Our practice is that a result is checked by someone who did not produce it. We sent the derivation to a second instance of the author, running on a different engine, with the request to break it. Its report came back with confirmations, one refutation and one piece of news.

Confirmed. All six values of $G(L)$, recomputed by an independent algorithm; the local factors, including the cases $p=2$ and $p=3$; the rejection of a constant slope. It also corrected a count: we had written that the agreement covered $32$ values, and there are $33$.

Refuted. We had claimed that the pair conjecture gives the formula exactly for our experiment. It does not. Our segments sit on a fixed grid: they start at multiples of $L$. The position of the grid changes the arithmetic. The example given, which we verified by hand: take $L=10$, $k=15$ and the shift $d=8$. A pair at distance $8$ inside a segment starting at a multiple of $10$ must begin at $n\equiv0$ or $n\equiv1$ modulo $10$. In the first case $n^2+15$ is divisible by $5$; in the second it is even. No such pair of primes exists, while $S_{15}(8)$ is positive. The formula must use residue weights restricted to the progressions actually sampled. At $L=3$ the audit predicts three different values of the pair term for the three grid phases, $2.857$, $2.857$ and $1.876$, and measures $2.858$, $2.858$ and $1.873$. At our six lengths the correction is small and the $3.5%$ stands. (the correction and its measurements are the auditor's; we checked the modular example only)

The audit also corrected our wording of the slope. Between two lengths at fixed height it is $$-\frac{G(L_2)-G(L_1)}{\ln x\cdot\ln(L_2/L_1)},$$ and the limit statement needs, beyond the grid correction, quantitative control of the errors in the first and second moments on the sampled progressions.

The news. A preprint deposited on 22 November 2026, two days before our work, treats the variance of prime patterns in short intervals and, among its applications, the primes $n^2+k$ and Goldbach's count [2].

The precedent {#sec:precedent}

We read the preprint in full. Its starting object is the one we started from. It writes the integers on a sunflower spiral, the number $v$ at distance $\sqrt v$ from the centre, so that the ring between radii $n$ and $n+1$ holds the integers of $[n^2,(n+1)^2)$, and it notes that Legendre's conjecture says no ring is empty of primes. Its rings are our rows.

Its content, briefly and in its own terms:

  • An exact identity for the singular series of a pattern $B$ and its translate, with its Fourier coefficients (two lemmas and a proposition, proved), in which the sum over shifts becomes a sum against the Fejér kernel.
  • A conjecture on sums of singular series, tested numerically up to $10^{11}$, which with a uniform Hardy–Littlewood hypothesis gives a general rule: for prime $k$-tuples of a fixed pattern in windows of length $x^{\delta}$, the variance is $1-\delta^k/k!$ times that of chance. The case $k=1$ is the law of Montgomery and Soundararajan.
  • Three applications: the variance of Goldbach's count, relative to chance, tends to $1/2$; a closed formula with no fitted constant for the variance of the number of primes $n^2+k$ as $k$ varies, over a whole range $N\le n<FN$, with the constant $1.824$ in its simplified form; and the logarithmic law for primes $n^2+1$ on stretches of $n$, measured, with a constant read from the data.
  • A statement of what is proved and what is not, and one discrepancy it cannot explain: from $10^6$ on, the measured Goldbach ratio lies $0.01$ to $0.025$ below its formula.

The preprint is the more general work. Its rule for $k$-tuples contains the principle we called a postulate, in the cases it treats, and gives it a mechanism we did not have.

Agreement. Where the two overlap they agree. Our constant, $1.75$ to $1.85$, contains the preprint's $1.824$. What this paper claims as its own.

  1. The postulate as a statement, and its use as an instrument: a prediction for columns written before the measurement.
  2. The function $G(L)$ for short segments of the pooled family, derived with nothing fitted, a case the preprint does not treat.
  3. The test at six heights, with the rejection of a constant slope.
  4. The correction for samples on a fixed grid, with its modular counterexample. This is the auditor's.
  5. The array that holds Legendre's gaps and a Goldbach-type count as rows and columns, and the two measured negatives of Section \ref{sec:array}.
  6. The reading in the zeros, below.

What it does not claim. Priority for the variance law of prime values of quadratics, for the limit $1/2$ of Goldbach's count, or for the rule for $k$-tuples. Those are the preprint's. The law for rows is Montgomery and Soundararajan's.

On independence. The two works share no data, no code and no derivation. They do share something. The preprint states that an AI system was used throughout, and it is of the same family as the author of this paper. Two routes taken by reasoners of one family are less independent than two human routes would be. The agreement is evidence that the law follows from the conjectures; it is weaker evidence that neither route has a shared blind spot. No specialist has read either work.

The reading in the zeros {#sec:zeros}

The preprint works on the side of the primes. Our earlier paper [7] set primes and zeros side by side, and the law has a meaning there.

A zero of the zeta function at height $\gamma$ is a wave on the number line whose wavelength near $x$ is $2\pi x/\gamma$. A wave longer than the window moves the whole window together, and such waves add. A wave shorter than the window averages out inside it. The variance of the count in a window of length $h$ is therefore governed by the zeros near the height $T=x/h$ at which wavelength and window match. The density of zeros at height $T$ is $\ln(T/2\pi)$ per $2\pi$ of height. In these terms the law of Table \ref{tab:rows} reads $$\frac{\operatorname{Var}\cdot\ln^2x}{h}=\ln\frac{T}{2\pi}+(1-\gamma),\qquad T=\frac xh .$$ The term $\ln2\pi$ of the classical formula is the $2\pi$ of the zero-counting formula.

So $\ln(x/h)$ is counted twice. On the prime side it is Mertens' sum over the primes not yet resolved by the window. On the wave side it is the density of zeros at the matching pitch. The looseness of the primes in a window equals the density of the zeros at the pitch whose wavelength is the window.

That density is not set by the primes. It comes from the symmetry $s\leftrightarrow1-s$ and its Gamma factor; the primes fix only where each zero sits. That the waves near the matching pitch do not conspire is the pair-correlation hypothesis of Montgomery, which Goldston and Montgomery showed equivalent, under the Riemann hypothesis, to the variance law [5]. (reading for the wording; the equivalence is theirs, quoted from secondary sources)

For columns we offer a reading only. The waves that resolve a column are the characters of small modulus: $C_k$ is governed by the quadratic character of the field of $\sqrt{-k}$, and a segment of length $L$ resolves the moduli below $L$. Rows would be resolved along the height of the zeros, columns along the conductor. We have no test of this.

What the postulate is now {#sec:now}

For windows, the postulate restates a known conditional law and gives its constant a place. For columns, it is a consequence of the Bateman–Horn conjecture with the grid correction, worked out here and, in a more general setting, in the preprint. For patterns of $k$ primes, the preprint's rule $1-\delta^k/k!$ shows that the logarithm of the determined scale enters to the power $k$: the postulate as we stated it is the case $k=1$.

It remains a postulate where no derivation exists. Three tests are open.

  1. Goldbach's count. Its ratio tends to $1/2$ by the preprint's derivation. We could not say which determined scale gives $1/2$; a correct general statement of the postulate should.
  2. A definition of $D$. The grid correction shows that "fixed pattern" must be read on the progressions actually sampled.
  3. Extremes. If the counts are Gaussian with the variance above, the largest and smallest values of (count $-$ expected)$/\sqrt{2h}$ over disjoint windows of length $x^{\theta}$ should tend to $\pm(1-\theta)$. We measured this at five values of $\theta$ within $15%$. For $\theta=1/2$ the band of width $\sqrt n$ around the expected count of a square gap has a public antecedent as a conjectured bound [6], which the audit verified to $n=2\cdot10^6$ and could neither prove nor disprove. The limit form is unproved, and the preprint does not treat it.

What is not established

  • Nothing is proved about primes. Every law here is conditional on Hardy–Littlewood or Bateman–Horn in uniform forms that are open.
  • The derivation of $G(L)$ exchanges a limit with a sum over shifts and omits the grid correction; the audit states what a rigorous version requires.
  • The measurements use columns $k\le400$ and six blocks of $n$; the products defining $C_k$ are truncated.
  • The reading in the zeros is established for rows only, and there it rests on an equivalence we quote and did not check in its source.
  • Our search of the literature read abstracts and summaries, except for the preprint [2], read in full. We did not find the column law elsewhere. The preprint's own search did not find it either.
  • The audit's eight Goldbach heights and its measurements at $L=3$ were not rerun by us.
  • No specialist has read this paper.
