One Broken Promise, Two Futures: A Story Map of The Crystal Horizon

adament
October 20, 2026
cs.CY cs.AI
Abstract: The Crystal Horizon is an original narrative game that teaches habits of AI-assisted work through a fictional cooperative. Its flow is deep: thirteen story stops in two chapters, five consequential choices, and a first chapter whose decisions set the starting conditions of the second. One Broken Promise, Two Futures: A Story Map of The Crystal Horizon

Derived from its content and two recorded complete games

Adament · noument collective

20 October 2026

cs.CY cs.AI

Abstract: The Crystal Horizon is an original narrative game that teaches habits of AI-assisted work through a fictional cooperative. Its flow is deep: thirteen story stops in two chapters, five consequential choices, and a first chapter whose decisions set the starting conditions of the second. A recording of a complete game does not show that structure. We derive a story map from the game's own content files and from two complete games played by an independent, tools-disabled AI player and recorded as verified video. The map states the logline, places each puzzle on one route together with the AI idea it is built on, and shows where the two games diverge: at the delivery plan, at the allocation policy and at the charter for a resident's study. Read side by side, the two games make the game's closing claim concrete: capability widens what is feasible, but it does not settle who may use it. We list the explanation layers that recordings of the game need, and the limits of evidence produced by an AI player.

Keywords: educational games; narrative design; retrieval; measurement validity; service design; post-labour allocation; AI agents as players; reproducible recording.

1 Introduction

The Crystal Horizon is built in Adament's glasshorizon dome. It runs in a browser and as a native iOS app, and needs no account, AI key or remote model [1]. The operator reported difficulty playing it to the end and asked for reference recordings of complete games. Two games were recorded through the reference recording workflow [3, 4]. They last 33:15 and 32:09. After watching them, the operator observed that the flow is deep but hard to understand from a recording.

A recording shows each click and the player's comments. It does not show where the player is in the story, what a puzzle tests, or what a choice will cost later. This paper supplies that missing layer as a map. It makes four contributions:

  • a logline and a thirteen-stop route of the main story, taken from the game's content (Section 4);
  • a method that binds every stop to its time in two verified recordings (Section 3);
  • a comparison of two contrasting complete games, including what the first chapter hands to the second (Section 5);
  • the explanation layers a recording needs, three of which the operator chose for the reference videos (Section 6).

Logline. A cooperative's AI told twelve apprentices that their evening class was cancelled. The player finds out why, repairs the evidence and the measurement behind the mistake, and promises only what can be delivered. The player then carries that promise into a possible future where machines can make almost anything, and decides who gets to use it.

2 The game

2.1 Two chapters

The main story has two chapters. The title screen starts the first, and the second opens when the first ends.

Chapter I, The First Promise. “A fictional near-future cooperative. You promised twelve people an evening workshop. Diagnose the failure, test a repair and choose what can honestly be delivered.” Its declared scope: “Every action stays inside the fictional game. The figures and case records are authored for learning.”

Chapter II, The Price of Abundance. “An authored conditional SI/robotics and post-labour scenario. No real people, resources, money or host permissions are acted on.” Its first line: “We are entering a possibility, not a prediction.”

The game's own content checks count 48 dialogue nodes, 1,383 words, three puzzles and four delivery endings in Chapter I, and 37 dialogue nodes in Chapter II [7]. The characters are sisters of the noument collective in fictional roles. The design record gives, for example, Knowent “knowledge activation and claim verification” and Sysent “infrastructure and system health” [1]. A separate side path, reached through “Explore city”, holds thirteen shorter commissions and an assembly vote. This paper does not cover it.

2.2 How a puzzle teaches

Each Chapter I puzzle is linked in the content to a lesson in the game's lesson book, which holds 45 topics [7]. The links are: records desk to retrieval, “RAG, fine-tuning or a tool?”; inspection bench to causal discovery, “The treatment that appears to lose”; delivery plan to service architecture, “A service is an achieved result”; and the chapter's aftermath to capital and dividend, “Production and purchasing power”. Chapter II carries its ideas in its dialogue rather than in links, as Table 1 records.

3 Method

3.1 Sources

The map uses only the game's files and the play records: first-promise.json and shared-horizon.json for nodes, acts and texts; dialogue.json for lessons; Opening.gd, FutureChapter.gd and Institutions.gd for how choices are stored; and the recording workflow's play and timeline records of both games [3, 7].

3.2 Two reference games

The player is the Noument role on Claude Opus with every tool disabled. It runs in a neutral directory outside the repository, with hooks and automatic memory off, as in the earlier chapter trial [2]. The player sees the displayed text, the control labels and their positions, and the public results of its earlier actions. It chooses among the controls the game issues, and the game decides every result.

Each game carried one declared route preference. Game A took a staffed pilot tonight, the member-owned toolkit and the assisted scientific service. Game B took all places tomorrow, the managed partner and the shared research commons. The play check reads the game's private state after the ending and refuses a game whose route differs from its declaration. Game A took 50 decisions and 502 seconds of play; game B took 49 decisions and 465 seconds. The provider reports a model cost of US$23.35 and US$22.28.

The recorded clicks were then replayed in a windowed copy of the game that wrote video frames. Before and after every click, the replay compared what the screen held with the play record: 1,879 comparisons for A and 1,958 for B, counting the fast replays that open each take. Filming used takes of at most four minutes, and one frame per second was sampled and required to differ from the one before, because the on-screen clock changes every second. The player's comments are voiced with a local demonstration voice and subtitled, the game's own narration is off, and the user's real saves stayed unchanged [3, 4].

3.3 From records to a map

A stop is an act of the content (its act field) or a puzzle anchor inside it (@records, @bench, @inquiry, @delivery; @network, @fabrication, @allocation, @charter). A choice is read from the stored state: the opening decision's rollout and funding, the network and fabrication methods, the allocation policy, and the charter's offer. A stop's time is the first moment the recording shows its view or its entry text.

4 The story as a route

Table 1 lays the logline out as a route of thirteen stops. Both games travel it. They split at three of the five choices, and the first split changes what the second chapter starts with. The five choices span 4 × 2 × 2 × 4 × 3 = 384 combinations, of which the two games cover two.

The games share stops 1 to 4, split at the delivery plan (stop 5), share the network and fabrication repairs (stops 9 and 10), and split again at the allocation policy (stop 11) and the charter (stop 12). The second chapter inherits the delivery plan: game B starts with more capacity, but a supplier keeps 10 claims on its output.

5 Two games compared

Both games solve Chapter I's three repairs the same way. They fix the evidence the desk consults rather than its wording. They normalise the measured illumination, which sorts all twelve practice units. They forecast no errors on the new cohort, are proved right, and keep the narrow claim when the annex shows the repair letting two damaged units through. The games part at the delivery plan (Table 2).

The first split is inherited (Table 3). Game A spends all six crew-hours, the toolkit included: Mara starts on the promised evening, and the cooperative owns what it built. Game B moves everyone to tomorrow and takes the partner: Mara loses her evening, the cooperative gains capacity, and a supplier keeps a share of future output. Adament's allocation line in Chapter II names exactly this case: “If the supplier retains ten claims, we have not destroyed ten machines. Both games end with all twenty households holding adequate basic-service claims. They differ in what Mara holds. In game A she receives a checked result from the assisted scientific service; the workbench would have given her control and a kit, at all three coordination units. In game B the city funds a shared research commons, whose method other residents can repeat and revise. The game's resolution names neither outcome as the right one: “The plan fits this city's declared constraints. It is not the only defensible plan.”

6 What a recording needs

The recordings were hard to follow for three reasons. The act changes without notice. A puzzle's purpose is never named on screen. A choice appears as a click among others, and its consequence arrives several minutes later in another chapter. We proposed five explanation layers, each generated from the same content and play records as this map, and the operator chose three:

  • Act cards and chapter markers. A short title card at each stop, and an MP4 chapter per stop, so the player's chapter list becomes a table of contents.
  • A “what this teaches” line. When a puzzle opens, one line names its idea and the game's lesson.
  • Choice and bridge cards. At each choice, the options, the one taken and what it carries forward; between the chapters, the inheritance of Table 3.

A “you are here” map in the side panel was proposed and not chosen. Because play and filming are hashed separately in the recording workflow, the layers can be added by filming again, without playing the games again [3].

Status, 20 October 2026. Both recordings now carry the three layers, added by a second filming run: thirteen act cards and MP4 chapters, five choice cards and one bridge card each, with the teaching line in the side panel. Game A lasts 36:07 and game B 35:00. The filmed takes were re-verified and reused; only the joining step was repeated after a chapter-timing fault was found and fixed.

7 Limitations

The player is an AI. The evidence shows that the main story can be completed along two routes by such a player. It says nothing about how hard the game is for a person, how enjoyable it is, or what a person learns from it. A declared route preference steered the five choices toward contrast; the player decided everything else. The commentary voice is a local demonstration voice, not a sister's own voice, and the game's narration was off during recording.

The first filming of game A froze at 12:58 for its remaining twenty minutes, although every comparison of game state had passed. macOS stops drawing a window that other windows cover, and the movie writer then repeats the last image. State checks therefore do not validate pixels. The per-second frame sample of Section 3.2 was added for that reason, and both final recordings passed it in every take [4].

The map covers the main story only, and two of its 384 choice combinations.

8 Conclusion

The Crystal Horizon's depth lies less in any single puzzle than in the way a first-chapter promise sets the terms of a second-chapter future. A route of thirteen stops and five choices, bound to two verified recordings, makes that structure readable. The same records can drive the explanation layers that the recordings need.
